# Forth TikTok: Rules

The standing rules for Yelden Sarybay's TikTok series, with the reason behind each one. Drawn from the project context and from the analytics of Episodes 1 to 3. Version 3, 5 November 2026: new sign-off; open loops, story props and zoom cuts. Version 2 (4 November) added the target audience and goal.

*How to use it: read sections 1 and 4 before every script, run the checklist before every post, and add a rule only when something has gone wrong or the data shows a pattern.*

## 1. Audience and goal

*Decided 4 November 2026. Every other rule serves this.*

**R1. The primary audience is small UK online sellers and would-be importers: people selling through TikTok Shop, Amazon, eBay, Etsy or their own site, or thinking about sourcing from abroad.**
Why: They are on TikTok in large numbers, they need exactly what Forth Import Export sells (sourcing and import management), and one of them becoming a client is worth more than months of view-based income.

**R2. The goal is enquiries for Forth first. Creator Rewards is a bonus, not a target.**
Why: Rewards needs 10,000 followers and 100,000 views a month before it pays anything, and it rewards broad reach that pulls the channel away from Forth. Enquiries have no threshold and can come at a few hundred followers.

**R3. About two in three videos are for sellers and importers; about one in three is a consumer story for reach.**
Why: Consumer stories (prices, Temu, shortages) travel further and teach TikTok who the account is for, while seller content earns trust with the people who might become clients.

**R4. Tag every story in the bank by segment before choosing it: shoppers (reach), sellers and importers (target), established SMEs (high value), professionals (LinkedIn, not TikTok).**
Why: It keeps the mix honest. Without tags the bank drifts towards dramatic news that entertains but never brings an enquiry.

**R5. Forth is named in the bio only: no Forth branding on screen, and no "DM me" or "search for my company" calls to action.**
Why: A hard sell kills the tone of a story channel. Anyone curious enough to open the profile sees the company name and can find it themselves.

## 2. Choosing a story

*The story is the vehicle; the trade process is what it teaches.*

**R6. Every video is built on a real, relatively recent news case.**
Why: Explainers get scrolled past. A real problem with real people ("a container is stuck and nobody will say why") gives viewers a reason to stay to the end, and the process is learned along the way.

**R7. Take stories from the news, not from Forth's own clients, and never name private individuals.**
Why: There are too few Forth cases to sustain a series, client work is confidential, and naming private people creates legal and reputational risk for no gain.

**R8. Sanity-check the premise before deep research: find out what happened after the famous moment.**
Why: Stories move on. The 2019 whisky tariff turned out to be a seven-year arc ending at zero in July 2026. Checking first avoids scripting a story that is already out of date.

**R9. Every story must teach one thing from the content pillars: a role, a platform, a nuance, or how a tariff reaches the shopper.**
Why: This is what makes it a series about how trade works, rather than a news recap. It also gives each video one clear takeaway.

## 3. Research and verification

*No primary source, no figure.*

**R10. Two research passes, then a primary-source check, producing a fact-check file before anything is filmed.**
Why: Press copies drift: "64%" was really "more than 60%", "93%" was "over 90%". Re-filming is expensive; checking is cheap.

**R11. Use primary sources only: gov.uk, HMRC, OBR, Federal Register, European Commission, WTO, company filings. Press articles are used only to find the primary document.**
Why: A figure under Yelden's name, and indirectly Forth's, must survive a challenge in the comments. The primary document is what you point to.

**R12. Give every claim a status in the fact-check file: Confirmed, Corrected, Not published, or Could not access.**
Why: It shows at a glance what is safe to say, and what must be softened or dropped.

**R13. If only press copies of a figure exist, attribute it clearly to the outlet or drop it.**
Why: The "£3bn of parcels from China" figure had no official source, so it was dropped rather than repeated as fact.

**R14. Check exactly what a figure counts: parcels vs items vs consignments, value vs count, nominal vs real, and what a label covers.**
Why: HMRC's "low value" series covers consignments of £873 or less from outside the EU. It is not the £135 relief, and treating them as the same would be a factual error.

**R15. Keep claims as cautious as the evidence, and the thesis claims as cautious as the thesis.**
Why: "No clear jump", never "no diversion". The thesis found no statistically significant average effect, which is not the same as proving nothing happened.

**R16. Separate who pays a tariff legally from who bears the cost.**
Why: The importer pays at the border; sellers and shoppers carry it through prices and lost sales. Getting this right is the core lesson of the series.

**R17. Forth Intel figures come only from the verified report data and its data rules, never from recall.**
Why: Any UK import figure shown under the Forth name must match the report canon, or it undermines the research business the series is meant to support.

**R18. Re-check dates and figures in the script on the day of filming.**
Why: The £135 deadline moved from March 2029 to October 2028 between drafting and filming Episode 2.

## 4. The hook: the first 3 seconds

*This is the most important section. The data says the door is the problem, not the room.*

**R19. The first five or six spoken words carry the conflict. No date, no name, no background first.**
Why: Episode 1 opened on "In April 2025, Britain was told...". Retention fell to 62% at 0:02 and 49% at 0:03, then declined only gently. Viewers decided in the first two seconds, before the tension arrived.

**R20. Be already talking at frame one. Trim every breath and pause before the first syllable.**
Why: Each silent fraction of a second at the start is time in which viewers swipe, and they do not come back.

**R21. Something on screen changes by 0:02: a headline, an object, a cut, or a punch-in on the face.**
Why: A static shot in a room gives the eye nothing new. Movement signals that the video is going somewhere.

**R22. Where possible, have a real object from the story in shot from the first frame (the glass, the charger, the parcel), not a generic prop.**
Why: A physical object gives the eye something new at 0:00 and ties straight to the story. Toy containers and gimmick props undercut the credibility the channel depends on.

**R23. Look straight into the lens, and choose a cover frame with eyes open.**
Why: Episode 1's first frame caught a downward look mid-blink. Eye contact reads as "this is for you".

**R24. The on-screen hook is a question with stakes, said and shown at the same moment, inside the safe area.**
Why: Episode 1's text sat so high that the top line was clipped. The text is the hook for anyone watching with the sound off.

**R25. The second sentence delivers the consequence the hook promised, inside the first 5 or 6 seconds. Backstory comes after.**
Why: Episode 3's new hook lifted retention at 0:03 to 63%, but most viewers then left at 0:04, because the next 16 seconds were background on loans and the WTO and the 25% tariff only arrived at 0:20. Pattern: hook, consequence, then "here's how".

**R26. Open a loop before the backstory: say what is coming and that it will be explained ("and the reason comes down to one line in a contract, which I'll get to"). Always pay it off, and never promise a cause the evidence doesn't support.**
Why: Episode 3 held 63% at 0:03 but lost most viewers at 0:04, when backstory began. An open question gives viewers a reason to sit through the context. Breaking the promise, or overclaiming the cause, costs trust.

**R27. Write the teaser in the last line so it stays true whatever the research finds.**
Why: The teaser is the reason to watch the next episode. Breaking it costs trust; Episode 2 honoured Episode 1's teaser.

## 5. Writing the script

*Written to be said aloud, the way Yelden speaks.*

**R28. Complete, flowing sentences joined with "while", "and", "which", "because". No fragments or one-word lines. Teleprompter lines break at breath points, but each sentence runs to its full stop.**
Why: This is Yelden's natural register, modelled on the AIFC news digest. Short broken lines (Episode 1) sounded staged. Tension should come from what is said, not from chopped delivery.

**R29. Follow the episode shape: hook, setup, the cast, the twist, why, takeaway and next.**
Why: It gives every video a forward pull: a problem, a surprise held back, a concrete reason, and one line the viewer can use.

**R30. Length 75 to 100 seconds, always over one minute: 260 to 280 words at about 175 words a minute.**
Why: The Creator Rewards Program only pays for videos of at least one minute, but every second past the minute must earn its place because completion rate drives reach.

**R31. Build in one "cut-first" sentence that can be removed in the edit without re-recording.**
Why: It gives room to trim to length, or to cut where the retention curve dips, without a reshoot.

**R32. Say the search terms out loud: tariffs, customs, import, shipping, HMRC, and the brand names in the story.**
Why: TikTok search counts. Episode 1 was already found by someone searching "china tariffs".

**R33. British English, pounds where possible, but the right currency for every figure. No em dashes, no diacritics.**
Why: The audience is UK. A wrong currency is a factual error: Episode 2 said "pounds" for a dollar figure and had to be re-recorded.

**R34. Flag illustrations as illustrations in the script.**
Why: The Episode 1 fridge example came from a retailer's comment, not the thesis. Marking it prevents it being presented as a finding.

## 6. Filming

**R35. Vertical, one take per script section, with a practice read to set the teleprompter speed.**
Why: The CapCut teleprompter does not follow the voice. Section takes make slips easy to re-record without redoing the whole video.

**R36. Deliver the twist and the takeaway still and straight to camera.**
Why: A change of energy marks the moment that matters and makes it land.

**R37. Face the light, and test the sound on earphones before the full shoot.**
Why: Poor sound is the fastest way to lose a viewer. In winter, finish outdoor shoots by mid-afternoon.

**R38. Grab a few 5-second location shots for cutaways.**
Why: They give the editor material to change the picture every few seconds.

## 7. Editing and inserts

**R39. Check the take against the script for slips in numbers, currency and names before editing.**
Why: Episode 2's "205 million pounds" and the auto-transcript's "Tim will build" were both caught at this stage.

**R40. Something changes on screen every 3 to 5 seconds; Yelden's face returns for the twist and the takeaway.**
Why: Visual change holds attention through the middle, while the face carries the moments of trust.

**R41. Use zoom cuts: punch in about 10% on a punchline or a new point, then cut back for the next sentence.**
Why: It changes the picture without needing a new insert, which keeps the 3 to 5 second rhythm on a talking-head shot.

**R42. Inserts go over the voice as cutaways, never as pauses of their own. Don't speed the voice up; trim pauses instead.**
Why: Pauses add time without adding information. Sped-up voices sound unnatural.

**R43. One message per chart, with units, currency and a source line on screen.**
Why: A chart viewers can't read in two seconds is noise. The source line shows the figure is real.

**R44. Keep text inside the safe area: about 150px from the top, 450px from the bottom, 140px from the right and 60px from the left of a 1080x1920 frame.**
Why: TikTok's interface covers the edges. Text outside this area is hidden or clipped.

**R45. Logos only to identify a company; original graphics rather than screenshots of apps or checkouts; headlines show outlet and date.**
Why: Avoids any suggestion of endorsement, and avoids reproducing others' material, while still showing the story is real.

**R46. Fix auto-subtitles: brand names, British spellings, currency symbols.**
Why: Wrong subtitles look careless and can misstate a figure for anyone watching without sound.

## 8. Posting

**R47. Before posting, choose a clean cover frame: eyes open, no subtitle over the view count, hook text readable.**
Why: The cover is what profile visitors see. Episode 2's cover had a subtitle sitting over the view count.

**R48. Write the caption with the key search terms and post the source list in the caption or a pinned comment.**
Why: It helps search and gives a ready answer to "where's that figure from?"

**R49. Keep the sign-off identical every time: "I'm Yelden. Follow for more real stories about how trade works."**
Why: It asks for the one action the account needs now: follows, which are reported to unlock the bio link at around 1,000. The bio ("I run Forth Import Export Ltd, helping businesses source and import. Here I tell real stories about how trade works.") names the company, so the spoken line doesn't need to. Repetition builds recognition.

**R50. Don't change the username (@yelden.trade) once the series is running.**
Why: Changes are limited to once every 30 days, and the old name is released to anyone straight away.

## 9. Measuring performance

*Two videos are not a pattern. Measure consistently and judge after five or six.*

**R51. Log every video at 48 hours and at 7 days: views, retention at 0:03, average watch time, % watched to the end, % For You, % UK, new followers, shares and saves.**
Why: Comparing videos at different ages is meaningless: a video posted yesterday hasn't finished its test batch. A fixed log turns noise into patterns.

**R52. The headline number is retention at 0:03. Target above 60%.**
Why: TikTok tests each video on a small batch of strangers and widens it only if they stay. Both early videos flatlined after about 5 hours, when the first batch swiped away.

**R53. Use the retention curve to find the exact second of any sharp drop, and match it to the script.**
Why: A drop at 0:02 is a hook problem; a drop in the middle is a section that runs long and should be cut next time.

**R54. Judge engagement as a rate (likes, comments, shares, saves divided by views), with shares and saves weighted highest.**
Why: Episode 2 had more comments and saves than Episode 1 on an eighth of the views, which suggests the content works once people stay.

**R55. Alongside attention, track fit: comments or DMs from sellers and importers, profile visits, and any enquiry that mentions TikTok.**
Why: Views measure reach; the goal is enquiries. A seller video with fewer views but a question from a real importer is worth more than a viral consumer story.

**R56. Don't draw conclusions about posting time until there are enough videos to compare.**
Why: At this size a handful of viewers swings the numbers. The hook explains far more of the gap between Episodes 1 and 2 than timing does.

## 10. Answering comments

**R57. Answer challenges from the fact-check file, at the same caution as the script.**
Why: "No clear jump in HMRC's figures so far", never "no diversion". Over-claiming in a reply undoes a careful video.

**R58. Don't put a single import figure on screen or in replies unless it is verified and labelled.**
Why: Overall imports moving is not the same as tariffed goods pulling ahead, and press figures often conflict with HMRC's own release.

## 11. Audience location

**R59. Build for a UK and Ireland audience; don't push the series to the Kazakhstan network.**
Why: Viewers were about half UK and a quarter Ireland on Episode 1, and two-thirds UK on Episode 3. TikTok has read the account correctly, and a large off-target audience would confuse that signal.

## Pre-post checklist

- Fact-check file complete; every figure Confirmed or Corrected
- First five or six words carry the conflict; no date or name first
- Talking at frame one; picture changes by 0:02
- Second sentence delivers the consequence by 0:06; backstory after
- Story tagged by segment; mix about two seller videos to one consumer story
- On-screen hook inside the safe area
- Take checked against script: numbers, currency, names
- Inserts or zoom cuts every 3 to 5 seconds; face back for twist and takeaway
- Open loop set up before the backstory, and paid off
- Sign-off word for word: I'm Yelden. Follow for more real stories about how trade works.
- Subtitles fixed: brands, British spelling, currency symbols
- Length 75 to 100 seconds, over one minute
- Cover frame clean: eyes open, nothing over the view count
- Caption has search terms; source list ready to pin
- Last line names the next episode, and the next episode will honour it
